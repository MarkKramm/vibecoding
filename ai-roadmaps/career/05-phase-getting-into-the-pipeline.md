## Goal of this phase

Phase 1 covered what employers are selecting for. Phase 2 built evidence a stranger can read. Phase 3 produced proof of skill before anyone paid you for it. Phase 4 turned that into an interview and a first ninety days. Every one of those is about **being worth hiring.**

This phase is about something else: **getting into the room.** A shortlisting decision, a referral, a recruiter's attention and a screening call are a different system from the work itself, and they behave differently. You can be excellent and still receive silence, because silence is the modal outcome of the pipeline and it carries almost no information about you.

**The uncomfortable thing about this subject is that nearly everything written about it is unverifiable.** You will be told a percentage of resumés are rejected by software, a number of seconds a recruiter spends on a scan, a multiple of improvement from a referral, an ideal length for a document. Almost none of it can be checked, the figures disagree with each other, and the people repeating them cannot tell you where they came from. This curriculum has a rule about that: mark unconfirmable claims as unconfirmable, and prefer "could not verify" to a confident guess. So this phase does not teach you those numbers. **It teaches you to build the only dataset that answers them for your situation, which is your own.**

**That reframing is the whole phase.** You cannot observe the hiring process from outside — you never see the other applicants, the internal candidate, the role cancelled three days after posting, or the parser that failed on your PDF. What you *can* do is instrument your side of it: log every application with its channel, record what came back and how long it took, change one variable at a time, and read your own rates honestly. That is the same discipline as taking measurements in the Cost track and predicting before testing in the Foundations track, applied to a system with a much worse feedback signal.

By the end you will have a dated log of real applications, a channel table with your own denominators, a parseability check performed on your own document, one job post read as a specification, a request for a range made early, and a written plan naming the single variable you will change next.

## Estimated time

**2 weeks** at 1–2 hours a day, 5 days.

Week 1 is the instrument and the document: the log, the parseability check, and the reading of a real posting as a specification. Week 2 is the channels: one person contacted about their work, one range asked for early, one variable changed deliberately and recorded before you start.

This phase has a longer clock than the others on purpose. **Nothing measured here produces a signal in a day**, and a two-week window is the shortest one in which a channel change can produce anything at all. If you have less time, do tasks 1, 3, 4, 12 and 15 — start the log, test your own document's parseability, compute your rates with their denominators, and write the plan. Those five carry the phase, and task 3 is the single highest-value hour in it.

If you are not job-hunting yet, do tasks 1, 3, 4 and 8 anyway. A parseable document and one genuine contact are assets that keep, and the parseability check costs twenty minutes.

- **A Markdown file or a spreadsheet for the log** — the one instrument this phase requires. A table with a header row is enough, and the file must live somewhere you will actually open.

Nothing here costs money, and this is the phase where that matters most. **A résumé service, a keyword-optimisation product or a paid application tool cannot sell you the one thing this phase produces**, which is your own response data with its denominator. Buying one mostly guarantees you never learn whether it worked.

## Lesson: An Unobservable System, and How to Instrument It

### Part 1 — Why this phase refuses to give you numbers

Start with what cannot be known from where you are standing. You never see the other applicants. You never see the internal candidate who was already the plan. You never learn that the role was cancelled, or filled by a promotion, or that the budget was frozen the day after it was posted. You never see the parser that dropped your document, and nobody emails you to say it happened. **And you never see a rejection you were not sent**, which is most of them.

That is not a hard problem, it is an impossible one: the process is unobservable in principle from the applicant's side, and the sample anyone writing about it has access to is their own experience. So the confident numbers you will be handed are almost all *survivor recollections* — someone's memory of what worked for them, converted into a rule and repeated until it sounds like a measurement. This curriculum's rule about unconfirmable claims applies to career advice exactly as it applies to benchmark quality and alignment risk: **mark it, do not launder it.** And notice what that does to a well-known genre of advice. A claim that most resumés are rejected by software, with no method and no denominator, is a claim about the world that nobody in the conversation has checked.

**What replaces those numbers is an instrument.** You can observe your own applications: what you sent, when, through which channel, and what came back. That is a small, biased, incomplete dataset — and it is *yours*, it has a denominator you control, and it is the only one that can ever tell you anything about your situation. Everything in the rest of this lesson is either a mechanism worth understanding or a discipline about how not to misread that dataset.

### Part 2 — Four ways a role reaches you

There are four broad routes into a role, and the useful thing about classifying them is that each has a different **cost, a different failure mode, and a different denominator.**

**Posted, and applied for.** The role is public and you submit against it. The cost per attempt is low, which means the volume of attempts is high, which means the denominator is large and the per-attempt signal is tiny. Failure modes: silence, or a rejection with no reason. This is the channel where people form the belief that nothing works, because it is the channel where almost everything is out of your sight.

**Referred by someone inside.** The mechanism here is not a favour, and understanding that is what makes the channel reproducible. An internal person who recommends you is transferring **a reduction in uncertainty**: they are telling the employer that a specific human they know will probably be competent, which is information the employer cannot get from a document. That is why referrals are worth more than their candidates usually look — and why they cannot be manufactured by asking strangers, because a stranger has no uncertainty to reduce about you. Part 4 is about building the contact that makes one possible.

**Inbound, because something you published was read.** Someone with the problem finds your write-up, your repository, or your answer to a public question, and contacts you. This channel inverts the usual order: the conversation starts with them already knowing one specific thing about you, which is exactly what a document cannot achieve. It is the reason Phase 2 exists, and its failure mode is that it takes months to begin.

**Sourced by a recruiter.** Covered in Part 3 as an incentive question, because the striking thing about this channel is how it behaves and why.

One consequence matters more than the rest. **The denominators differ between these channels**, so a comparison of raw counts between them is meaningless: you might send forty applications and write three messages, and if one of the three produces a conversation, that is not "messages are better than applications, 1 in 3 versus 0 in 40" — it is two different sizes of sample, measuring two different things. The honest way to compare them is to hold the effort constant: how many conversations did each channel produce per hour you spent? That is a number you can actually compute, and almost nobody does.

### Part 3 — Your document meets a machine before it meets a person

This part is deliberately careful, because it is where the unverifiable numbers live.

**What is knowable.** Extracting structured fields — name, contact details, roles, dates, education — from an arbitrary PDF is genuinely difficult, and it is difficult because documents are arbitrary: two columns, tables, a contact block in an image, dates formatted four different ways. That is a property of parsing, not a claim about any employer's tooling. And **the failure is silent.** A parser that cannot locate a section does not raise an error and does not email you; it produces an empty or wrong field, and the resulting record is short by whatever it could not read.

**What is not knowable, and why it does not matter.** Which employers run a parser, how good it is, and whether it ever touched your document. You cannot determine any of that from outside, so the prevalence question is unanswerable — and it is also irrelevant, because the remedy costs about twenty minutes and is good practice regardless: **make the document boringly parseable.** One column. Standard section names. No text inside images. No contact details in a table or a header box. Consistent date formats. All of it is a cost paid once, that makes the document readable by a machine *and* easier for a person.

**The string-matching part, stated honestly.** If a filter is a literal string match, then synonyms fail it — "customer support" does not match "customer service" as a substring, and no amount of clever formatting changes that. The response is not to stuff keywords. It is narrower and it is a matter of truth: **use the employer's own nouns where they are also accurate about you, and nowhere else.** If the posting says "incident response" and you did incident response, write their phrase rather than your paraphrase. If the posting says "Kubernetes" and you have never used it, no wording makes that true, and closing a real gap with vocabulary is a thing you cannot do. That boundary is the entire discipline, and it is why task 5 exists: separating a wording gap (free, fixed by rewriting) from a real one (not free, fixed by work).

**And test it yourself, because you can.** Take the exact file you send and extract its text. If your own copy-and-paste loses the phone number, drops the last role, or interleaves two columns into unreadable soup, you have just seen what a machine sees. That check is the highest-value twenty minutes in this phase, and it requires no statistics about anyone's tooling.

### Part 4 — Referrals as an information problem

If a referral's value is a reduction in uncertainty, then the question is not "how do I get one" but **"how do I become known to someone who is already inside?"** That reframing produces a plan instead of a hope.

The mistake almost everyone makes is the first message. It asks for time, an opinion, a referral or a job, from a person who has never heard of you — which costs them something immediately and gives them nothing. **The move that works is the specific reply to published work:** a real question about a thing they actually wrote, showing that you read it, asking about what it does not say. You are not asking for anything. You are demonstrating the one property a referral has to transfer — that you are competent and specific — using their own subject as the evidence.

**The escalation order matters.** Contact, then a conversation, then a question about their team, then a request. Asking at step 1 is the standard failure, and it fails for the reason any premature request fails: the other person has no basis to say yes, so the honest answer is no.

**And be honest about the base rate: most of these do not lead anywhere.** A person may not reply. They may reply warmly and then vanish. There may be no opening. None of that is a verdict on you, and converting it into one is how people abandon the only channel with a real mechanism behind it. What you are accumulating across a dozen attempts is not favours, it is **a small number of people who know what you can do** — which is the asset, and it compounds.

### Part 5 — Reading a job post as a specification

A posting is a document written by a busy person, sometimes with a template, sometimes by someone who does not know the stack. Read as a specification rather than as a gate, it tells you three useful things.

**Its first three requirements are the role's real shape.** Those are what the team is actually short of, in the order the author thought of them. The tail of a long list is usually aspiration: the wish-list of a perfect candidate who does not exist, included because there is no cost to writing it down.

**Which requirements are hard filters and which are preferences.** A hard filter is usually checkable and disqualifying: a specific tool with no substitute, a licence, a location, a clearance, a language. A preference hedges its own language — "familiarity with", "exposure to", "nice to have", "or equivalent experience" — and those hedges are informational: the author is telling you the line is soft.

**And what is boilerplate.** Paragraphs about the company's mission, its funding, and its culture are the same paragraphs in every posting, and they predict nothing. Skip them, then return to the first three requirements and apply the gap test from Part 3: for each one, is there anything in your material that a reader would see as evidence? **If there is no evidence, that is a gap and not a wording problem.** Writing around a missing skill produces a document that fails the first technical question in the interview, which is a worse outcome than a rejection at the reading stage.

One more reading, which is the practically useful one: **look for what the team is short of rather than what the title says.** Two postings with the same title can want different people — one wants someone to build the thing, one wants someone to keep the thing running — and the difference is visible in which nouns repeat.

### Part 6 — The offer, and the honest limits of leverage

Negotiation advice is usually written for people with leverage. Here is the version that survives without it.

**Ask for the range early**, in the first real conversation, before a long process has made asking feel expensive. If the answer is evasion, you have learned something about the employer while it is still cheap. Phase 1's research is what tells you whether the number is sane.

**Get the offer in writing.** A verbal number is a mood; a written one is a position. Ask for it in writing with the components itemised, and read it rather than reacting to it.

**Do not negotiate against yourself.** The failure mode is a paragraph of apology and justification ending in a hedge, which invites the employer to help you lower your own number. Name one figure, give one reason grounded in what you will be doing, and stop talking. Silence is uncomfortable and it is not your job to fill it.

**Deadlines are commonly soft, and you cannot assume it.** If you need time, ask for a specific number of days rather than letting the date pass in silence — an expiring offer is usually a negotiating position rather than a fact, but the only way to find out is to ask.

**Now the honest part.** With no competing offer and no scarce skill, your leverage is thin, and pretending otherwise produces a worse outcome than accepting a fair first offer. The reason not to obsess over the last increment is structural: **a first role's main product is the evidence it generates for your second one.** Portfolios are built from work you were trusted with, and the difference between one increment and the next is small compared with the difference between having shipped something real and not having. Optimise for what you will get to build, what you will be allowed to write down, and who you will work next to — and revisit the number from a position that has evidence behind it.

**And never inflate a competing offer.** Software work is a small community, references circulate, and the discovery cost is permanent. It is the one move in this phase with a downside that does not expire.

### Part 7 — The instrument, and how to read it without fooling yourself

The deliverable of this phase is a log with a header row and honest entries, and the discipline is in how you read it.

**Log the channel, always.** Date, channel, role, what you sent, days to first response, and what the response was. The channel column is the one that changes your behaviour, because it is the only column that lets you compare *routes* rather than accumulate *effort*.

**Compute rates with their denominators, and refuse to compute them on small samples.** Four applications and one response is not a 25% response rate; it is four observations. At that size the number can only mislead you, and the specific way it misleads is that it will make you abandon a channel or commit to one for no reason at all. Set a threshold for acting before you have the data — ten per channel is a reasonable starting point — and until then treat the log as a record rather than a result.

**Change one variable at a time, and write the plan before you start.** If you change your document, your channel and your targeting in the same fortnight, the next two weeks teach you nothing, because you cannot attribute whatever happens. This is the same single-variable discipline as the Cost track's caching experiments, and it fails in the same way when ignored.

**Redact before you publish.** The log is about you, and it is also a record of other people's hiring processes and correspondence. Employment names, recruiter names and anything a person told you in confidence come out. Under a statute like RA 10173 the other people in your log are data subjects, and Phase 4 of the safety track is the reason a published version should contain channels, dates and outcomes rather than identifiable people.

**And read a quiet fortnight correctly.** Silence is the modal outcome and it is nearly information-free: you cannot observe the denominator, so you cannot tell a weak application from a filled role. The only honest reading of a zero-response period is that a channel has not yet produced a signal — not that you are not working, and not that the channel does not work. That is why the plan has a duration and a threshold attached, decided in advance, rather than a feeling.

## Common Pitfalls

**Repeating an unverifiable hiring number because it is memorable.** A percentage with no method, no denominator and no date is not evidence, and quoting it in an interview or a write-up costs you credibility with the one person in the room who knows the field.

**Reading application volume as effort rather than as a signal about the channel.** Forty applications producing one conversation and three messages producing one conversation are not the same result, and only the per-hour version of that comparison is honest.

**Comparing response rates across channels with different denominators.** A rate computed from forty attempts and a rate computed from three are not comparable, and the smaller one will always look more dramatic in whichever direction it happens to point.

**Computing a rate from four observations and acting on it.** At that size the number is noise, and the specific harm is that it will make you abandon a channel that was working or commit to one that is not.

**Assuming a parse failure will be reported to you.** It will not. Nobody tells you that your document came through with a missing section, because from the employer's side nothing failed — they simply have a shorter record.

**Chasing keywords you cannot honestly claim.** A real gap closed with vocabulary fails at the first technical question, which is a worse outcome than a rejection at the reading stage and a worse use of everyone's time.

**Fixing your document for a machine and ruining it for a person.** The recommendation is a boring single-column layout with standard headings, which is also easier to read. If a change makes it harder for a human, it is the wrong change.

**Asking for a referral in the first message.** A stranger has no uncertainty about you to reduce, so there is nothing for them to recommend. The escalation order — contact, conversation, question, request — is not etiquette, it is the mechanism.

**Reading a recruiter's silence as a judgement of you.** An agency recruiter is usually paid on placement by the employer, so their attention follows the role's economics rather than your ability. A junior role carries a smaller fee and therefore less follow-up, and that is arithmetic rather than assessment.

**Treating one recruiter's account of their process as the industry's.** It is one data point from one person, possibly at a company unlike the one you are applying to. Record it and label it.

**Negotiating with an apology.** A hedged paragraph invites the employer to help you lower your own number. One figure, one reason, then silence.

**Inflating a competing offer.** In a small field, references circulate and the discovery cost does not expire. It is the one move in this phase whose downside is permanent.

**Publishing the log as it stands.** It is a record of other people's processes and correspondence. Employers, recruiters and confidences come out before anything goes public.

**Believing a zero-response fortnight is feedback about you.** You cannot observe the denominator, so a quiet period is a channel without a signal yet rather than a measurement of your worth.

## Quiz

### Q1. You send forty applications and get one screening call. A friend sends three messages to people who wrote things they liked and gets one conversation. What is the correct conclusion? <!-- id: cr-05-getting-into-the-pipeline-q01 energy: high -->

- [ ] Messages work about thirteen times better than applications, so stop applying
- [ ] Applications do not work, and the forty attempts were wasted
- [x] The two counts rest on very different denominators and measure different things, so the honest comparison is conversations produced per hour spent on each channel
- [ ] Both results are noise and no conclusion of any kind is available

**Why:** Comparing one response from forty attempts with one from three produces a ratio that looks dramatic and means almost nothing, because the effort, the volume and the target population all differ. Holding effort constant is what makes the comparison meaningful, and per-hour is the version you can actually compute from a log. The fourth option overcorrects: the results are small, which is a reason to keep recording rather than a reason to conclude nothing at all.

### Q2. A posting lists "Kubernetes" among its requirements and you have never used it. What is the right response? <!-- id: cr-05-getting-into-the-pipeline-q02 energy: high -->

- [ ] Add it to your skills list, since you can learn it quickly enough to be honest
- [x] Treat it as a real gap rather than a wording gap, and either address it with work or apply knowing that this requirement is unmet
- [ ] Rewrite it as a related technology you have used, so the string match still fires
- [ ] Omit the application, since a single unmet requirement disqualifies you

**Why:** Where a requirement is accurate about you, using the employer's own noun is free and correct; where it is not accurate, no wording makes it true and the gap will surface at the first technical question. A real gap is closed by work, not by vocabulary, and knowing which kind you are looking at is the discipline this phase teaches. A single unmet requirement on a wish-list is usually not disqualifying either — the first three requirements are the role's real shape, and that is the part to judge yourself against.

### Q3. You receive an offer by phone and want time to consider it. What is the most defensible action? <!-- id: cr-05-getting-into-the-pipeline-q03 energy: normal -->

- [ ] Accept immediately, since the offer may be withdrawn if you hesitate
- [ ] Say nothing and let the stated deadline pass while you decide
- [ ] Ask a friend to negotiate on your behalf so you do not appear difficult
- [x] Ask for the offer in writing and for a specific number of days, because a verbal number is not a position and deadlines are commonly softer than stated

**Why:** A written offer with itemised components is something you can read rather than react to, and asking for a defined number of days is a normal request that is far better than silence. Letting a stated date pass without asking is the one option that genuinely risks the offer. Deadlines are frequently negotiating positions rather than facts, but that is a thing to test by asking rather than to assume by waiting.

### Q4. You are about to change your application document, start applying to a different kind of role, and switch job boards — all in the same week. Why is this a problem? <!-- id: cr-05-getting-into-the-pipeline-q04 energy: high -->

- [ ] It is not a problem; changing more things at once produces a stronger signal
- [x] Changing three variables at once makes the next two weeks uninformative, because nothing that happens can be attributed to any one of them
- [ ] Job boards do not affect outcomes, so only the document matters
- [ ] It wastes effort, but the log will still show which change worked

**Why:** Attribution requires a single changed variable, which is why the log has a channel column at all. Change three things and the following period is a recorded outcome with no cause attached to it — the same failure mode as changing a caching setting and a prompt at once. The log cannot rescue this, because the log records what happened rather than why.

### Q5. Where does a referral's value actually come from? <!-- id: cr-05-getting-into-the-pipeline-q05 energy: high -->

- [ ] It bypasses the assessment, so the candidate's ability matters less
- [x] The internal person transfers a reduction in uncertainty about you, which is information an employer cannot get from a document
- [ ] It is a favour owed to the person who refers, and the employer is honouring it
- [ ] It proves the candidate has a network, which is itself the qualification

**Why:** An employer reading a document has no way to know whether the claims are accurate, and a recommendation from someone they trust replaces that gap with a judgement from a source they have reason to believe. That is why the channel cannot be manufactured by asking strangers — a stranger has no uncertainty about you to reduce, which is what makes the escalation order in this phase a mechanism rather than a courtesy.

### Q6. Your log shows eight applications this fortnight and no responses at all. What is the most accurate reading? <!-- id: cr-05-getting-into-the-pipeline-q06 energy: normal -->

- [ ] The channel does not work and should be abandoned
- [ ] Your document is broken and needs rebuilding
- [x] The channel has not yet produced a signal, and you cannot observe the denominator, so the honest move is to continue to the threshold you set in advance
- [ ] Eight applications is a large enough sample to conclude you need a different approach

**Why:** Silence is the modal outcome and you cannot see how many of those roles were already filled, cancelled or had an internal candidate, so a quiet fortnight is nearly information-free in both directions. That is exactly why the plan's threshold and duration are written before the data arrives — a decision made in advance is a plan, while the same decision made in response to a quiet week is a reaction to noise.

## You're ready to move on when...

You keep a dated log of what you actually sent and through which channel, and you can state your own response rate per channel with its denominator — and you decline to draw a conclusion when the denominator is too small. You have extracted the text from your own application document and seen what a machine sees, and you have fixed what failed to survive. You can read a posting as a specification, mark its requirements by kind, and say which gaps in your evidence are wording and which are real. You have contacted one person about their published work without asking them for anything. You have asked for a compensation range early, and you know what you are optimising for in a first role besides the number. And you have written down the single variable you are changing next, with the threshold that would change your mind.

## Free vs Paid

### What's free is enough

Every mechanism in this phase is free. The log is a table. The parseability test costs one extraction and twenty minutes. A job board account is free and Phase 1 already has one. Reading a posting as a specification needs only the posting. Contacting a person about their published work needs only that person's published work and a message, and it is the highest-value action in the phase. Your rates are computed from your own entries with a spreadsheet. **The one thing money cannot buy here is the dataset** — you have to run the process to get observations, and no product shortens that.

### What a paid tier adds

Paid job-board tiers mostly sell you volume and visibility, which is exactly the variable this phase teaches you to hold constant while you measure another one. A subscription that puts you in front of more postings increases the size of your denominator without improving your rate, and if you buy it at the same time as you change your document, you have made the next month unreadable. The genuinely useful paid things are narrow: a domain for the portfolio that inbound depends on, and possibly a professional association or community membership where the practitioners in your target area actually are.

### When it's worth paying

Pay for a domain if you are serious about the inbound channel, because a link you control is what makes published work followable. Pay for one community membership if the people you need to be known by are demonstrably in that room and nowhere else — and then participate rather than lurk, because a membership you do not use is a subscription to hope. **Do not pay for a résumé service, a keyword-optimisation tool or an "AI application" product in this phase.** Each of them inserts a change into a process you are trying to measure, from a vendor whose claims you cannot check, at the exact moment you started keeping the log that would have told you whether it worked.
