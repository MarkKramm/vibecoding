# Study Rules

The anti-burnout rules for this curriculum. **Read this before any phase.**

This is not a motivational preamble. It is first because the most common way a self-study plan fails is not running out of material — it is running out of the person. A curriculum that assumes perfect weeks produces a learner who quits in week three and concludes they were not cut out for it, when the actual cause was a plan with no slack in it.

---

## The rules

### 1. Five days a week is the plan, not seven

The schedule assumes **five focused days**. Saturday is optional catch-up and Sunday is off. Two rest days are not a reward for finishing — they are part of the method. Consolidation happens during rest, and a plan with no rest days has no mechanism for it.

If you study seven days a week you will feel excellent for about two weeks, and then you will stop entirely. **The failure is scheduled by the plan, not caused by you.**

### 2. A missed day is data, not a verdict

Missing Tuesday tells you one thing: Tuesday was a bad day to study. It does not tell you anything about your ability, your discipline, or your future in this field.

The failure mode to watch for is **the spiral**: miss a day, feel bad, avoid the material because it now carries guilt, miss a week, conclude the project is dead. The break is the missed day. The spiral is what actually ends the project.

**Rule:** if you miss a day, the next session starts exactly where the last one ended. No compensatory doubling. No restarting the phase.

### 3. Lower the bar before you lower the frequency

If a day does not have room for the full session, **do a smaller session rather than no session.** Read one section. Run one command. Take the quiz.

A 15-minute session keeps the thread intact; a skipped one means tomorrow starts with re-orientation. The thread is worth more than the minutes.

### 4. Do not read a phase without building its deliverable

Every phase has a `## Deliverable / proof of work` section. That section is not a suggestion appended to the lesson — it *is* the phase. Reading without building produces the specific and dangerous state of recognising a technique without being able to use it, which feels like knowledge and behaves like its absence.

### 5. Stop when the phase says stop

Every phase has a `## You're ready to move on when...` section. When you meet it, move on. Extra time on a phase you already understand is the most comfortable form of procrastination available here, and it costs you the phase you have not started.

### 6. Your own words, or it did not happen

When you take notes, write what you understood, not what was written. If you cannot restate a mechanism without looking, you have not learned it yet — you have successfully copied it.

This is the single highest-value habit in this curriculum, and the one most often skipped because copying feels like progress.

### 7. One thing at a time

This curriculum has nine tracks. Do not read four at once. Depth in one beats familiarity with five, and familiarity with five is what you get by trying to do four at once.

The exception is deliberate pairing: a mechanism track with a practice track, using different parts of your attention. Reading Prompting alongside Vibecoding Craft is a reasonable pairing. Reading Retrieval, Agents, Finetuning and Internals simultaneously is not.

### 8. Free tiers are the plan, not a compromise

Every tool this curriculum names has a free path. Where a paid tool is genuinely better, the phase says so and names the free alternative.

**Do not spend money in the first month.** Every paid thing you buy before you understand what it does is a purchase you would make differently after Track 7. There is no technique in the first six tracks that requires a paid plan.

### 9. When the field moves, keep the layer

Model names, prices and leaderboards change on a scale of weeks — this curriculum says so on its first page. When something you learned becomes outdated, check which layer it was:

- **Mechanism** — attention, tokenization, the KV cache, chunking, evaluation. Durable. It does not go stale.
- **Specific** — a model name, a price, a context size. Volatile by design, and always dated where it appears.

**If you learned the mechanism, being wrong about the specific costs you nothing.** That is the whole reason this curriculum is structured the way it is.

### 10. Comparison is not information

There will always be someone who started earlier. Their position tells you nothing about your own, and their timeline in a field this new is not a measurement of anything.

## The productive-struggle rule

There is one place where the usual advice inverts.

**When a hands-on task is going badly, stay with it longer than is comfortable.** Struggling to make something work, and then understanding *why* it failed, is where most of the learning in this curriculum actually happens. That discomfort is the signal that something is being learned, not that something is wrong.

**But set a limit and name it out loud.** "I will give this 45 more minutes, then look at the answer." Unbounded struggle is not productive, it is just demoralising. The difference between the two is whether you decided in advance.

Then, when you do look something up, **close the loop**: go back and reproduce the fix yourself, from nothing. Reading the answer is not the same as having found it, and the gap between those two is where the false confidence lives.

## A weekly self-check

Answer these on Friday. Three sentences each, written down.

1. **What did I actually build this week?** Not read — built.
2. **What did I have to look up twice?** That is the thing to review.
3. **Did a quiz question surprise me?** Surprise is the most useful signal this curriculum produces. Follow it back into the lesson.
4. **Am I on pace, behind, or ahead — honestly?** Behind is fine and normal. Behind *and pretending otherwise* is what causes the spiral.
5. **What is the one thing I will do first on Monday?**

## When to take a real break

Take a week off, deliberately and without guilt, if **two or more** of these are true:

- You have opened the material three days running and not actually read any of it
- You are re-reading phases you already completed instead of starting the next one
- Building the deliverable feels like a chore you are avoiding rather than a task you are doing
- You are studying to avoid feeling behind rather than because you want to know the thing

That last one is the real warning. **Studying from anxiety rather than curiosity does not survive contact with a long curriculum.** A planned week off costs you seven days. An unplanned collapse costs you the project.

---

## What this curriculum promises, and what it does not

**It promises:** that the mechanisms in it are accurate and explained at the level where you can predict their behaviour; that every tool named has a free path; that the volatile claims are dated and marked; and that the paths are honest about what they cost in time.

**It does not promise:** that it is complete — nothing in this field is, including this week's documentation; that any specific model, price or technique will still be current a year from now; or that finishing it makes you an expert. It makes you **literate**, which is the prerequisite, and there is a great deal of difference between those two things.

That difference is the subject of the entire last track.
