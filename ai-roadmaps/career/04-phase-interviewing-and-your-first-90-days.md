# Phase 4 — Interviewing and Your First 90 Days

## Goal of this phase

Convert the evidence you have built into a conversation you can survive, and then into a plan for the first three months after someone hires you.

Phases 1 to 3 built the case: what employers are pricing, the portfolio that answers it, and the proof of skill you accumulate when nobody has hired you yet. This phase is the last mile. It covers the interview itself — what is actually being tested, the four questions to have ready answers for, debugging out loud, AI tool use in a take-home, the questions you ask — and then the ninety days after the offer, which is where a hire is confirmed or quietly regretted.

The organising idea is a continuation of the track's thesis rather than a new one. Phase 1 established that **an employer is buying down the risk of hiring you**, and that the risk they are pricing is whether you can be trusted with a system. The interview is the last instrument in that process, and it is almost entirely a test of **reasoning** rather than recall. Nobody in an AI interview is impressed that you have heard of retrieval-augmented generation. They are trying to find out what you do when you do not know something, because in this field everyone does not know something most of the time.

The second half of the phase is the part candidates neglect and employers do not: the first ninety days. A portfolio is a claim about your capability, and a job is where that claim gets tested against reality. This phase gives you a dated plan so that the first month is spent learning deliberately rather than reacting.

By the end you will have: a spoken answer to four questions about one project, a rehearsed out-loud debugging habit, a written position on AI disclosure, a question list for the room, and a dated ninety-day document you could start using the week you are hired. That is the practical end of this track.

## Lesson: Interviewing and Your First 90 Days

### Part 1 — What an AI-role interview is actually testing

Start with the thing that determines everything else, stated as reasoning rather than as a rule about how every company operates.

**The through-line of the whole track applies to the interview directly.** An employer is buying down the risk of hiring you, and the risk they are pricing is whether you can be trusted with a system. So the interview is not primarily a knowledge check. It is a series of attempts to observe **how you reason when you are not certain**, because every real system will put you in that position within the first month.

That leads to an expectation list rather than a topic list. Expect some form of each of the following:

**Explaining your own past work, under questioning.** Usually the largest part, and where a portfolio becomes a liability or an asset. They are not asking you to present. They are asking you to go deeper than your writeup, in a direction you did not choose.

**Reasoning about tradeoffs.** Why this approach rather than another, what it cost you, what you would give up for something else. Anyone can describe a system they built. Only someone who made the decisions can say what the alternatives were.

**Debugging a described scenario, out loud.** "Imagine this system returns the wrong document for a query it handled correctly yesterday. Walk me through how you would approach it." They are not testing whether you name the right cause. They are testing whether you have a process and can narrate it while thinking.

**Estimating something.** Order-of-magnitude reasoning about cost, latency, storage or volume. The purpose is not the number. It is whether you can decompose an unfamiliar problem into quantities you can reason about, and whether you notice when an answer is obviously implausible.

**How you would evaluate it.** This is the one that has become decisive, and the reason is structural rather than fashionable. Generation became cheap; verification did not. Every organisation that ships a model-based system now has the same problem, which is that output is fluent whether or not it is correct. **"How would you know it works?"** is the question that separates candidates now, because it is the question the employer cannot answer for themselves and the one most candidates have never been asked.

Note the shape of that list. Four of the five items are impossible to answer well from memorised knowledge, and the fifth — estimation — rewards a habit rather than a fact. **This is why interview preparation for this field is mostly the work of understanding your own projects rather than the work of acquiring facts about the field.**

One honest framing note. This describes what the interview is *for*, derived from what the employer is buying, and it is not a measured claim about what any particular company asks. Processes vary widely, some are badly designed, and some are mostly a culture fit conversation. **Unverified** as an empirical claim about the distribution of interview formats. It is sound as reasoning: if the risk being priced is trust with a system, then reasoning under uncertainty is what a competent interviewer would try to observe.

### Part 2 — The four questions to be ready for

These are Phase 2's defence questions, and this phase's job is to turn them from a written audit into four spoken answers. Pick **one project** — the one you would lead with — and prepare a specific answer to each.

**1. Why did you choose this approach?**

Not what you used. Why. The answer is only visible against the alternative you did not take, so name it: "I used retrieval rather than fine-tuning because the policy changes monthly and the answer needs to cite its source." If the honest answer is "it was what I knew," say that and then say what you would do differently now. That is a better answer than a fabricated rationale, and an experienced interviewer can tell the difference.

**2. What is a tradeoff you accepted?**

This is the highest-signal answer of the four. A tradeoff proves that a decision existed and that you were the one who made it. Speed against accuracy, cost against latency, a simpler design that handles fewer input types. Name the thing you gave up and say why giving it up was correct for this problem. A project with no tradeoff usually means someone else made the choices.

**3. Describe a bug you found, and how you found it.**

This one cannot be bluffed, because it asks about an event. What did you expect, what happened, how did you notice, what did you change? The "how you found it" half is the part that matters — anyone can report a bug, and noticing one requires having looked. If you have kept running notes per Phase 2, this answer is already written down and only needs saying aloud.

**4. What does your project do badly?**

The limitation, stated without hedging. "It works on PDFs with a text layer; I did not handle scanned documents." Note what that sentence demonstrates: it proves you tested the boundary of your own claim. A candidate who cannot name a limitation has either not tested or is unwilling to say, and both are worse answers than a small honest one.

**Why one project rather than five.** Five vague answers are five chances to be caught, and they share the same defect: none of them is deep enough to survive a follow-up. One project with four specific answers survives several layers of questioning, because the specifics are connected to each other and to real events. If the interviewer moves on to a second project, you answer as far as you honestly can and then say where the edge of your knowledge is. That is not a weakness in the interview; it is the behaviour being screened for.

**Prepare these aloud, not on paper.** Write them first, then say them without reading, then have someone interrupt you mid-answer with "why?" The written version and the spoken version are different skills, and the interview tests the second.

### Part 3 — Debugging out loud

Interviewers frequently care more about the process than the answer, and this is the single most trainable skill in the phase.

**The structure to narrate.** Say what you think is happening, say what you would check to confirm or rule it out, say what you would expect to see if you are right, and say when you are guessing. Four beats, in that order, repeated as the scenario develops. It sounds mechanical written down; spoken aloud it reads as competence, and its real value is that it keeps you moving instead of freezing.

**Say the hypothesis out loud even when it is probably wrong.** A wrong hypothesis that you then rule out with a stated check is a good answer. Silence while you search for the right answer is a bad one, because the interviewer learns nothing about your process — which is the only thing they can actually assess from a described scenario.

**Name the specific check, not the category.** "I would add logging" is weak. "I would take the query that failed and run it against the retriever directly, with the same parameters, and see whether the right document comes back at all — that separates a retrieval problem from a generation problem in one step" is a strong answer, because it is specific and because it is designed to discriminate between two explanations.

**Say when you are guessing.** "I am not sure whether their index is rebuilt on write; if it is not, I would expect a lag, and I would check the write path first." Labelling your own uncertainty is not a concession. It is precisely the discipline Safety & Ethics Phase 3 teaches with measured, inferred and believed, and it is what makes your confident statements worth trusting.

**The strong version of not knowing.** "I do not know, and here is how I would find out" is a strong answer, and it is worth understanding why. It demonstrates three things at once: that you can recognise the edge of your knowledge, that you have a method for extending it, and that you are not going to invent an answer in front of a person who will later depend on you. In a role where being trusted with a system is the thing being assessed, those are the three most relevant signals available in one sentence.

**The failure mode is pretending to know.** It is a specific and expensive mistake. A confident wrong answer is detectable, and once detected it contaminates everything else you said, including the parts you got right — because the interviewer can no longer tell which of your statements are checked and which are performance. The asymmetry is stark: an honest gap costs you one question, and a bluff costs you your credibility for the rest of the interview.

**Practice this aloud.** Take a system you built, describe a plausible failure in it, and narrate your way through the four beats while someone listens. Do it three times on three different scenarios. It is uncomfortable and it works.

### Part 4 — Using AI tools in a take-home without hiding it

This is the honest-practice question of the phase, and it is worth a position rather than a feeling.

**The position, stated plainly.** Using AI tools in a take-home is generally fine unless you have been told otherwise, and the actual risk is not the use. **The risk is undisclosed use that you cannot defend.** If you submit work you cannot explain, the interview exposes it, and the exposure is worse than never having submitted — because it converts a neutral candidate into one who misrepresented their work, and interviewers talk to each other.

**Why the tool itself is not the problem.** The scarce thing in this field is judgement and verification, not generation. An employer who understands that will care about what you decided and how you checked it, not about whether a model produced the first draft. What they cannot accept is a submission where nobody made the decisions — because then the artefact is not evidence about you, and the take-home has tested nothing.

**Three practical rules, and they cover most of it.**

**Disclose the use.** Say what you used it for, briefly and without ceremony, in the README or the covering note. "I used an assistant to draft the initial structure and the test scaffolding; the retrieval design and the evaluation are mine, and I can walk through any part of it." One sentence removes the entire risk category.

**Be able to explain every part.** The test is not "did you write this by hand" but "can you defend this line, this parameter, this choice." If there is a section of your submission you would have to look up in order to explain, either go and understand it now or remove it. This is the same standard Phase 2 applied to portfolio items, and the same reason: an item you cannot explain is a liability rather than an asset.

**Never let the tool make a decision you cannot justify.** Generation is fine; delegation of judgement is not. Choosing the metric, deciding what counts as correct, accepting a limitation, deciding what to cut — those are the decisions the take-home exists to observe. If a tool picked them for you, you have submitted someone else's judgement with your name on it.

**Policies differ between employers, and asking is legitimate.** Some organisations permit declared AI use freely, some restrict it in take-homes specifically to see your unaided work, and some prohibit certain tools for data reasons. The policy is usually stated in the instructions, and if it is not, asking before you start is a normal question rather than an admission. **Unverified** as to how common each kind of policy is; there is no reliable public data on the distribution, and it is changing quickly. The correct action does not depend on that answer — read the instructions, and ask if they are silent.

**The distinction that matters most.** A take-home with disclosed, explainable AI use that you can defend is a stronger submission than one written unaided and poorly reasoned, because the employer is buying judgement and you demonstrated it. A take-home with undisclosed use you cannot defend is worse than not applying. Those two outcomes are separated by one sentence of disclosure and an hour of genuinely understanding your own submission.

### Part 5 — "What would you do differently?", and the questions you ask

**The question is a gift.** "What would you do differently?" reads like a trap and functions as an invitation. It asks you to demonstrate judgement about your own work, which is precisely what the whole process is selecting for, and it is one of the few questions where a thoughtful answer cannot be penalised.

**Why it is not a trap.** A candidate who says "nothing, it worked" has answered a different question — whether the project succeeded — and missed the one asked. Everyone who has built something knows what they would change. Answer with the specific change: what you would do, why, and what it would buy you. "I would build the evaluation set before writing the retrieval code rather than after, because I spent two days tuning against examples I had already seen" is a complete and strong answer, and it also happens to be true of most people's first project.

The same question in its other common form is **"what would you do differently if you had another month?"** Same answer, larger scope. The temptation is to describe a grander system. The better answer names the one change with the largest effect, which demonstrates prioritisation rather than ambition.

**Now reverse it, because the interview is bidirectional.** You are also gathering evidence, and the questions you ask reveal the team's actual state. A team that cannot answer a question about its evaluation process either does not have one or does not want to discuss it, and both are useful to know before you accept.

**Four strong questions, and what each is really asking.**

**"How do you decide something is good enough to ship?"** This asks whether a shipping bar exists and whether it is written down. A team that describes a specific threshold with a name attached has one. A team that says "when it feels ready" does not, and that tells you what your first months will feel like.

**"What does your evaluation process look like?"** The most directly informative question you can ask in this field, and the one most likely to produce an honest answer, because it is a factual question about their practice rather than a judgement about them.

**"What broke recently, and what changed afterwards?"** This is the best single question in the list. It asks for a real incident, and the second half — what changed — tells you whether the team learns from failures or absorbs them. A team that names an incident and a process change is a team you can learn in.

**"How does the team use AI tools in its work?"** Ask this neutrally rather than as a position statement. The answer tells you the actual norms, which as a new employee you will be expected to follow rather than set.

**Weak questions, and why.** Anything answerable from the website — what the company does, how big it is, what the culture is — wastes the one chance you have to learn something not published. Anything about perks before an offer exists signals the wrong priority, and it is information you can obtain later. And a question asked to appear interested rather than because you want the answer is usually detectable, which costs you more than silence would.

**Two practical notes.** Write the list before the interview, and take it in with you — asking from a written list is normal and reads as prepared. And when they answer, ask one follow-up about the answer rather than moving to your next question. The follow-up is where the interview becomes a conversation, and a conversation is what a competent interviewer is trying to have.

### Part 6 — Salary, negotiation, and rejection

**Salary, at a high level and without invented numbers.**

**Do not name a number first if you can avoid it.** The first number stated anchors the conversation, and the employer's range is information you do not have. A workable response when asked early: "I would rather understand the role properly first — what range have you budgeted for this position?" If they insist or the process requires a figure, give a range rather than a point, and give one you have actually researched rather than one you have guessed.

**A range based on research you have actually done.** The research is: the same role, the same seniority, the same engagement type, in the market you would be working for. Note that engagement type changes the comparison substantially, which Phase 1 covered — a contractor rate and an employee salary are not the same quantity, because one includes contributions and protections the other does not.

**Total compensation is more than base.** Benefits, contributions, paid leave, equipment, whether they pay for the tools you need, whether the engagement is employee or contractor, and what the currency and payment handling look like. For remote work from the Philippines specifically, the fees and exchange-rate handling can be a meaningful fraction of the money, and those are normal things to clarify rather than awkward ones.

**Mark the market figures unverified, because they are.** This curriculum does not print salary numbers. **Unverified** for any specific figure you encounter, including any you find in a survey — read who published it, what they sell, and what population they sampled. What this phase gives you is the procedure: research your own market, avoid naming a number first, compare total compensation rather than base, and treat the published number as a hypothesis to be tested rather than a fact.

**Handling rejection, mechanically rather than motivationally.**

**Most rejections carry no information.** They are the result of a comparison you cannot see, a budget change, an internal candidate, a role that was already filled, or a hiring manager's arbitrary preference on a Tuesday. Treating an uninformative rejection as feedback is a category error, and it is the mechanism by which a job search damages people who are perfectly competent.

**A specific rejection is valuable, and you should recognise it.** "We needed someone with more production deployment experience" is information. It names a gap, and a named gap has a response: go and get that experience on a real project, or aim at roles where it is not the bar. Most rejections do not contain this, so when one does, write it down and act on it.

**The correct response to silence is volume plus iteration, not self-assessment.** Silence is the default outcome of most applications and it is not data about you. What actually moves a search is applying to more roles and changing the thing that is not working — the framing of the project, the roles being targeted, the answers in the interview. Self-assessment in the absence of information produces only the decision to stop, which is the one change that cannot help.

**One honest note about the market.** Response rates vary enormously by market, role and time, and **Unverified** as to any specific figure. The practical implication does not depend on the figure: because you cannot control the rate, you control the volume and the quality of what you send, and you insulate your self-assessment from a process that mostly returns noise.

### Part 7 — The first 90 days, as a dated plan

The interview gets you the job. The first three months decide whether it was a good hire, and this is the part almost nobody plans.

Structure it in three periods with different goals. Write the dates in, because a plan without dates is a list of intentions.

**First two weeks: learn, and ship something tiny.**

The goal is orientation, and the specific mistake to avoid is trying to prove yourself by taking on something large immediately.

- **Learn the codebase, the deployment process, and who to ask.** Not everything — the shape. Where does the code live, how does a change get to production, who reviews what, and who actually knows how the ambiguous parts work. The deployment process in particular is the thing that will embarrass you if you learn it in an emergency.
- **Ship something tiny, in week one or two.** A one-line fix, a documentation correction, a small test. The purpose is not the value of the change. It is to walk the entire path — branch, review, test, deploy — while the stakes are zero, so that you know the path before you need it.
- **Write down every question and everything that confused you.** This is the highest-value habit in the entire phase and the one with the shortest window. You will never again have the outsider's view: within a month, the confusing things will look obvious to you and you will no longer be able to see them. That record is not a diary — it is a map of what the onboarding documentation is missing.
- **Ask the questions rather than sitting on them.** A new hire asking basic questions in week one is expected. The same questions in month six are a problem, and the reason is not patience — it is that by then you will have been assumed to know.

**Days 15–45: take one thing end to end.**

- **Take one real piece of work through the whole cycle, including its evaluation.** Note the second half, because it is the part people skip. Shipping a feature without measuring it means you have not demonstrated the thing this field actually needs — that you can tell whether it works.
- **Find the thing everybody works around, and understand why.** Every team has one: the flaky test, the manual step, the table nobody trusts, the script that only one person can run. Understanding why it exists teaches you more about how the team really operates than the documentation does, and it is often the thing you end up fixing.
- **Start documenting.** Where does it go? Into the onboarding gap you have been recording since week one. This is the first place where your outsider's perspective is worth something to other people rather than only to you.

**Days 46–90: own something, and ask for feedback explicitly.**

- **Own something.** A component, a pipeline, an evaluation suite, an area of the product. Ownership means you are the person who knows its state and who is expected to notice when it breaks. This is the transition from doing assigned work to being responsible for an outcome, and ninety days is a reasonable horizon for the first version of it.
- **Propose one improvement, with evidence.** Not a list of suggestions — one change, with the measurement or the incident that justifies it. This is the same discipline as your portfolio: claim, evidence, stated limitation. It also demonstrates the thing that gets people promoted later, which is noticing something and being able to show it matters.
- **Ask for feedback explicitly rather than waiting for a review.** Do not wait to be told. "I want to check how this is going — is there anything I am doing that you would want done differently?" is a normal question and it is almost never asked. It also puts you on the right side of the most common new-hire failure, which is finding out in a formal review that something has been wrong for two months.

**The capture habit, restated because it is the phase's practical core.** Write everything down in the first month — every question, every confusion, every undocumented step, every "why is it like this". Two reasons. The first is that the record becomes the documentation contribution that earns you credibility early, because you are the only person who can currently see the gaps. The second is that it doubles as your own study guide: the things you had to write down are the things you did not know, and by day 90 you should be able to answer most of them.

### Part 8 — The AI-specific risks of the first ninety days

**Over-claiming early is the specific risk, and it is worth naming precisely.** A portfolio is a claim about your capability, and if it implied more than you have, the job is where that surfaces — not out of malice, but because real work reaches the edges a portfolio never tested. The portfolio was built on your data, your scope, and your time. Production has someone else's data, an unfamiliar codebase, a deadline, and a system that other people depend on.

**Why this happens to honest people.** Not usually through deliberate misrepresentation. It happens because a portfolio project you finished six months ago looks more capable in retrospect than it was, because you described it at the level of the best case, and because you were selected partly on the strength of that description. You arrive with a reputation slightly ahead of your actual ability, which is an uncomfortable position and an extremely common one.

**The recovery, and it is the important part.** Close the gap fast and ask for help early. That means: identify the specific thing you claimed and cannot yet do, work on it deliberately in the first weeks rather than hoping the topic does not come up, and when you hit the edge of your knowledge on real work, say so to the person who can help rather than bluffing to the person who cannot. Asking for help early is a strength signal in a first job. Asking for help in month five about something you were assumed to know in week two is not.

**Do not bluff, and this is mechanical rather than moral.** In a first job, bluffing has a short half-life and a large blast radius: the person you misled will find out, the work will carry the defect, and the discovery will recast everything you said before it. The cost of an honest gap is one conversation. The cost of a bluff is your credibility with the people who decide whether you stay.

**The measured, inferred, believed discipline, applied to work.** Safety & Ethics Phase 3 gave you a habit of labelling your own confidence, and it is the most practically useful thing you can carry into a new job. In practice it sounds like: "I measured this on 200 examples and the accuracy is 0.87." "I infer that it degrades on longer inputs, because I saw it twice, but I have not tested it." "I believe the caching is why the second call is fast; I have not confirmed it." Three sentences, three different confidence levels, and a colleague can now tell which of your statements to act on — which is worth more than any of them being confident.

**Working with AI tools as a new employee, where the norms are set by the team.** Your personal position on AI tooling is not the one that applies. Ask what is permitted before you assume, follow the team's disclosure norms even where they are stricter than you would choose, and note that in some workplaces the restriction is not stylistic at all — it is about data. Pasting client data, internal code, or personal information into an unapproved tool is a data-handling incident with your name on it, not a matter of taste. Phase 2's secret-hygiene material and the Safety & Ethics track's privacy material both stop being theoretical the moment you have an employer's data.

**One framing note that ties the phase together.** Phase 1 said the scarce thing is judgement, verification and accountability. The first ninety days are where you demonstrate all three under someone else's constraints: judgement in how you prioritise, verification in how you check your own work, and accountability in how you behave when something you did turns out to be wrong. That is what the ninety days are being assessed for, and it is the same thing the interview was assessing. Nothing about the standard changes when you are hired. It just stops being hypothetical.

## Common Pitfalls

**Memorising answers instead of understanding the project.** A rehearsed answer collapses at the first follow-up, and the follow-up is the whole interview. The four answers to prepare are specific to a project you actually built, which is why they survive questioning that a question bank does not.

**Preparing vague answers for five projects rather than specific answers for one.** Five shallow answers are five separate chances to be caught, and none of them goes deep enough to demonstrate anything. Depth on one is the Phase 2 claim, restated in a different room.

**Bluffing when you do not know something.** The most expensive single move available to you. It is detectable, and once detected the interviewer cannot tell which of your other statements were checked. "I do not know, and here is how I would find out" costs you one question and demonstrates the three things being screened for.

**Staying silent while you think.** In a debugging scenario, silence is indistinguishable from having no process. Narrate a hypothesis even when it is probably wrong, and rule it out with a stated check.

**Treating the interview as one-directional.** You are also deciding, and the questions you ask are how you find out what the team's evaluation practice, shipping bar and incident history actually are. A candidate who asks nothing has learned nothing and signalled nothing.

**Asking questions answerable from the website.** It wastes your one chance to learn unpublished information, and it reads as unprepared rather than interested. Ask about shipping criteria, evaluation, recent failures, and how the team uses tools.

**Naming a number first in a salary conversation.** It anchors the negotiation with information you do not have. Ask for their range, and if pressed, give a range you have actually researched rather than one you have guessed.

**Comparing a contractor rate to an employee salary directly.** They are not the same quantity, and the difference is the contributions, protections and fees each one does or does not include.

**Treating an uninformative rejection as feedback.** Most rejections are about comparisons you cannot see, budgets, or timing. Reading them as a verdict on your ability produces the decision to stop, which is the only response that cannot help.

**Trying to prove yourself in week one by taking on something large.** The correct first move is a tiny shipped change that walks the whole path — branch, review, test, deploy — while the stakes are zero.

**Not writing down your questions in the first month.** The outsider's view expires within weeks and never returns. The record is both your study guide and the raw material for the documentation contribution that earns you credibility early.

**Waiting to be told how you are doing.** A formal review two months late is the most common new-hire failure. Ask explicitly and early; the question is normal and almost nobody asks it.

**Over-claiming and then bluffing to cover it.** If your portfolio implied more than you can do, the job is where it surfaces. Close the gap deliberately and ask for help early. Bluffing has a short half-life and recasts everything you said before it.

**Assuming your personal AI tool norms apply at work.** Ask what is permitted and follow the team's disclosure norms. In some workplaces the restriction is about data handling rather than style, and pasting employer data into an unapproved tool is an incident rather than a preference.

## Quiz

### Q1. In an AI-role interview, what is the interviewer mainly trying to find out? <!-- id: cr-04-interviewing-and-your-first-90-days-q01 energy: normal -->

- [ ] Whether you know the current frameworks and model APIs
- [x] Whether you can be trusted with a system, which is observed through how you reason when you are uncertain
- [ ] Whether your opinions about AI match the team's
- [ ] Whether you can generate working code quickly under time pressure

**Why:** The track's frame from Phase 1 carries into the room: the employer is buying down the risk of hiring you, and the risk is your judgement on a system nobody can fully specify. Recall and generation both became cheap, so an interview that tested them would be testing the wrong thing. That is why four of the five things you should expect — explaining past work, tradeoffs, debugging out loud, evaluation — cannot be answered from memorised knowledge.

### Q2. You are asked a technical question and you genuinely do not know the answer. What is the strongest response? <!-- id: cr-04-interviewing-and-your-first-90-days-q02 energy: high -->

- [ ] Give the most plausible answer you can construct and commit to it
- [ ] Say you do not know and wait for the next question
- [x] Say you do not know, then describe how you would find out and what you would expect to see
- [ ] Redirect to a topic you know well

**Why:** Admitting the gap demonstrates that you can recognise the edge of your knowledge, which is the precondition for anyone trusting your confident statements. Describing how you would find out demonstrates method, and naming what you would expect demonstrates that the method is specific rather than a gesture. Bluffing is the expensive option: once one confident wrong answer is detected, the interviewer can no longer tell which of your other answers were checked.

### Q3. What makes a debugging-out-loud scenario go well? <!-- id: cr-04-interviewing-and-your-first-90-days-q03 energy: normal -->

- [ ] Naming the correct root cause as quickly as possible
- [ ] Asking the interviewer for more information before committing to anything
- [ ] Listing every possible cause of the symptom in order
- [x] Narrating a hypothesis, the specific check that would test it, what you would expect to see, and saying when you are guessing

**Why:** The scenario is usually described too thinly to have one certain cause, so the interviewer cannot be scoring the answer — they are scoring the process. A wrong hypothesis ruled out by a stated check is a good answer, because it shows a working method. Specific checks are what discriminate: "run the failing query against the retriever directly" separates two explanations, while "I would add logging" does not.

### Q4. You used an AI assistant substantially while completing a take-home. What is the defensible approach? <!-- id: cr-04-interviewing-and-your-first-90-days-q04 energy: high -->

- [ ] Rewrite the submission by hand afterwards so the use is not visible
- [x] Disclose what you used it for, make sure you can explain every part, and remove anything whose decisions were not yours
- [ ] Do not mention it, since the tool is now a normal part of the work
- [ ] Refuse to use any AI tool, since take-homes are meant to be unaided

**Why:** The risk is not the tool, it is undisclosed use you cannot defend — the interview will expose it, and the exposure recasts you as someone who misrepresented their work. Disclosure removes that entire category, and explainability is the real test: if you cannot defend a parameter or a design choice, that part is not evidence about you. Policies differ between employers, **Unverified** as to how commonly, so read the instructions and ask when they are silent.

### Q5. Why is "what would you do differently?" a gift rather than a trap? <!-- id: cr-04-interviewing-and-your-first-90-days-q05 energy: normal -->

- [ ] Because it is an easy question that buys time for the harder ones
- [ ] Because admitting fault makes you look humble and likeable
- [x] Because it invites you to demonstrate judgement about your own work, which is what the whole process is selecting for
- [ ] Because it lets you describe a much more ambitious version of the project

**Why:** Nearly every other question asks you to report on something. This one asks you to evaluate it, which is the skill the phase keeps returning to. Answering "nothing, it worked" answers a different question — whether the project succeeded — and misses the one asked. The strongest form names a specific change with a reason and what it would buy, for example building the evaluation set before tuning on the same examples you later measure against.

### Q6. Which question is most likely to tell you something useful about a team you might join? <!-- id: cr-04-interviewing-and-your-first-90-days-q06 energy: normal -->

- [ ] What is the company culture like?
- [ ] How large is the engineering team and how is it organised?
- [ ] What are the opportunities for growth and promotion?
- [x] What broke recently, and what changed afterwards?

**Why:** It asks for a real incident, and the second half of the question — what changed — reveals whether the team learns from failures or absorbs them. It also cannot be answered from the website, which is the test for whether a question is worth spending. Questions about culture, size and growth are answerable from published material and, in an interview, mostly fill time that could have produced information you cannot get elsewhere.

## You're ready to move on when...

- You can explain **what the interview is testing** and why it is reasoning rather than recall.
- You have **specific spoken answers** to why this approach, what tradeoff you accepted, a bug you found and how, and what your project does badly — all about one project.
- You can **debug a described scenario out loud**, with hypotheses, specific checks, expectations, and honest labels on your guesses.
- You can say **"I do not know, here is how I would find out"** in a way that sounds like competence rather than retreat.
- You have a **defensible position on AI tool use** in a take-home, and you have removed or learned anything in your best submission you could not explain.
- You have a **question list** that would produce information, and you know why the weak questions are weak.
- You can hold the **salary conversation** without naming a number first, and you know why total compensation is not base.
- You can state your **response to rejection** without treating an uninformative rejection as a verdict.
- You have a **dated ninety-day plan** with an artefact per period, and you know why the first month's notes are the contribution.

This is the last phase of the track, and the standard does not change after it. **The interview and the first ninety days are the same assessment conducted at different levels of access** — first they ask how you reason about a system, then they watch you do it on theirs. Nothing in this phase is preparation for a performance that ends at the offer.

## Free vs Paid

**This phase costs nothing, and the expensive version of it would actively hurt you.**

**What free gives you, and it is all of it.** Your own project is the raw material. A Markdown file holds the answers, the question list and the ninety-day plan. Your phone's recording app is enough to rehearse aloud. The interview practice that matters — saying the four answers without reading them, and narrating a debugging scenario while someone interrupts — costs only an hour and a willing friend or a recording.

**What paid options would add, and why they are the wrong purchase here.** Interview-prep courses and question banks teach you to recognise questions rather than to reason about systems, which is the opposite of what the interview measures; a memorised answer collapses at the first follow-up. Mock-interview services do provide a real second reading and an unfamiliar questioner, which is genuinely useful — but the free substitute gets most of it, which is asking anyone who will listen to interrupt your answers with "why?" Paid salary data is convenient and often more granular than free sources, and it is not what stands between you and a reasonable range: the procedure in Part 6 is the substance, and the number is a hypothesis either way.

**The one cost that is not free, and it is the whole phase.** Rehearsal time, and specifically the time spent saying things aloud that you would rather only write down. Writing the four answers takes an hour; saying them until they survive an interruption takes several, and it is the part that determines whether the interview goes well. That is the correct allocation, and it feels like the wrong one because writing produces a document and speaking produces nothing you can look at. **The interview happens in the spoken version**, which is why the unglamorous half of this phase is the half that matters.
