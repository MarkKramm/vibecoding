# Career & Getting Hired — Track Overview

> **Status: not started.** None of this track's four phases are written yet. This overview describes what the track **will** contain, based on the agreed plan. There is no phase content to read today.

## What this track is for

Skill and employability are not the same thing. The other nine tracks build the capability; this one builds the **evidence** of it.

That difference is the entire reason this track exists. You can finish this curriculum able to build and evaluate real systems, and still be invisible to anyone hiring, because nobody knows what you did, why you made the choices you made, or whether you can tell a good result from a bad one. This track is about closing that gap deliberately rather than hoping it closes on its own.

The track's core claim is short and it is the argument for everything in it: **one deep project with an evaluation suite beats five demos.** A hiring manager cannot tell five tutorial clones apart from five other people's tutorial clones. They can tell, immediately, whether you measured something and admitted a limitation.

There is a second, harder-edged subject here. The field is noisy, titles are inflated, and a great deal of advice about getting into AI work is written by people selling something. So this track teaches you to **read a job post for what it actually requires**, and it names the roles a beginner can realistically enter — evaluation and data work being the most accessible, research and frontier training the least.

## Who this suits

**Anyone finishing this curriculum who wants the skill to turn into work** — which, given where this project started, is the intended reader.

It is written for someone in the Philippines working remotely, on a $0 budget, with no professional network in the field and no prior industry job to point at. That is not a limitation the track apologises for; it is the situation the advice is designed around, including timezone reality, contractor versus employee engagements, and payment and tax at a high level.

**Read this track early if you are job-hunting now**, not at the end. Its phases tell you what evidence to accumulate *while* you learn, which is far cheaper than reconstructing it afterwards. The portfolio discipline in Phase 2 is something to run in the background from your first deliverable, not something to do in a fortnight at the end.

## What you need before starting

- **Some completed work.** The portfolio phase assembles deliverables you have built. With nothing built, it has nothing to assemble.
- **A GitHub account**, and git installed.
- **No programming prerequisite beyond what the other tracks gave you.**
- **Roughly 1–2 focused hours a day, five days a week.**

Nothing here costs money. Portfolio hosting, publishing and open-source contribution are all free, and the track is explicit that buying a course or a certification is not what is standing between you and a job.

## What you will be able to do at the end

- Explain what employers are actually selecting for now, and why judgement and verification matter more than generation.
- Name the roles that exist around these systems, and state honestly which ones a beginner can enter and what the barrier is for each.
- Read a job post and identify what it actually requires, separate from title inflation.
- Describe what remote work from the Philippines realistically involves, including engagement type and payment.
- Assemble the deliverables you already built into a portfolio rather than starting from nothing.
- Write a project writeup that states the problem, the approach, what broke, how you knew it worked, and what you would do differently.
- Make a project runnable on a clean machine, with pinned dependencies and no secrets.
- Apply the "could I defend this in an interview?" test and cut anything that fails it.
- Contribute to an open-source project honestly and at an appropriate scale.
- Write a technical explanation of a mechanism you learned, and use writing as a way to find your own gaps.
- Build something for one real user.
- Explain your own past projects under questioning, including their limitations.
- Produce a dated ninety-day plan that starts the day you are hired.

## Roughly how long it takes

**4 phases, about 4–5 weeks at five sessions a week** once the phases are written — **but the portfolio work runs alongside everything else**, and that is the point.

Read the track in four or five weeks. Act on Phase 2 across the whole curriculum. If you are job-hunting now, the job-search material in Phases 1 and 4 is actionable immediately.

## What being on a $0 budget costs you here

This track is the one where budget genuinely restricts options, and it is written to be honest about that rather than cheerful.

**What costs nothing and carries most of the weight:** a GitHub portfolio, a written project with a real evaluation suite, an open-source contribution, a technical post explaining a mechanism you learned, and a project built for one real user. **These are the things that actually get people hired at junior level**, and all of them are free.

**What you genuinely give up:**

1. **Paid credentials and bootcamps cannot be your route.** Certificates carry little weight in this field relative to demonstrated work, and this curriculum's position is that the money is better spent later, on the experiment that changes a decision.
2. **Conference and in-person networking.** Travel and events cost money you do not have. The free substitute is writing in public and contributing to open source, which build a visible trail without travel.
3. **The strongest compute for portfolio projects.** You cannot train a large model to show off. You do not need to — evaluation work, retrieval systems and well-measured small fine-tunes are all achievable at zero cost and are more legible to a hiring manager than an unmeasured large one.
4. **Time-zone overlap with some markets.** Philippine time overlaps well with Australia and reasonably with parts of Europe and the US west coast's morning, but not with all of it. The track states this as a practical constraint to plan around rather than a barrier, because remote-first employers increasingly hire on output rather than hours.

**One honest caution, and it is the most important line in this overview:** free tiers are for *learning*, and their data terms are often not appropriate for **client or employer work**. If you take on paid work handling someone else's data, the Safety & Ethics track's privacy material stops being theoretical. Read it before you take the work, not after.

## How this track connects to the others

**Before it:** everything, in the sense that this track assembles what the others produced. Phase 2 in particular has nothing to work with until you have built deliverables.

**Safety & Ethics Phase 5 hands off to this track deliberately.** That phase ends the learning phase and states you are ready; this track owns everything about getting hired and the first ninety days. The boundary is intentional — do not expect the two to repeat each other.

**Finetuning & Evals is disproportionately important here.** Its evaluation material is the most directly employable skill in the curriculum and the most accessible junior entry point. If you are reading this track with a job in mind and skipped that one, go back.

## The honest caveat

**Job-market claims are volatile, and this track handles that by teaching procedures rather than statistics.** You will not find salary figures here, or "AI job growth" percentages, or lists of companies hiring. Those numbers are stale within months and are usually quoted by someone with an incentive.

What you get instead is a procedure: read the post for X, ask this question in the interview, test the project this way, ask for this in an engagement. Procedures survive a changing market; statistics do not.

The durable content — that judgement is what became scarce, that one measured project beats five demos, that evidence is built by writing and building rather than by claiming — will still be correct when the specific job titles have been renamed twice.

That single paragraph is the shape of everything Phase 2 teaches, and starting it now means you are accumulating evidence rather than reconstructing it.
