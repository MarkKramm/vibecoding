# Phase 5 — Distillation and Small Models

## Goal of this phase

Phase 1 concluded that fine-tuning is usually the wrong answer, and named the narrow conditions where it is right. **Distillation is the case that satisfies them by construction**, which is why the track ends its technical arc here.

The idea is simple. You have a system that works — a strong model doing a job, measured by an eval suite you already built. Most of its traffic is the *easy* majority: routine cases the strong model handles correctly and expensively. Distillation takes that working system and transfers its behaviour into a small model you can run cheaply, so the easy majority stops costing full price.

What makes it satisfy Phase 1's conditions is that the preconditions come free. **Volume** exists by definition, because you are distilling a workload. **Stability** is established, because the system already works. **Data** is generated by the teacher rather than collected. **Evaluation** already exists, because Phase 4 insisted you build it before claiming anything worked. That is why distillation is the strongest economic argument for training at all — and the reason this track does not end at "usually do not fine-tune."

Two techniques share the name, and knowing which you are doing matters:

| | **Classical KD** | **Sequence-level / data distillation** |
|---|---|---|
| Trains on | The teacher's full output **distribution** — the probability over all tokens | The teacher's **generated sequences**, as ordinary supervised examples |
| Signal | Richer: "this was 70% likely, that 20%" | Sparser: "this is the answer" |
| Needs | Access to the teacher's logits | Only text, so it works with **any API** |
| In practice for LLMs | Rare — it requires model internals | **This is what almost all LLM distillation is** |

The distinction is worth internalising because most writing about distillation describes classical KD, while almost everything practitioners do is the sequence-level kind. If your teacher is a commercial API, you only ever have text, so you are doing data distillation whether or not that is what you called it.

By the end you will have generated a dataset from a teacher, filtered and verified it, trained a student, and evaluated it against **both** the teacher and the untuned student — and you will have stated the terms-of-service position for your teacher, because that is a contractual question rather than a technical one.

## Lesson: Turning an Expensive System into a Cheap One

### What distillation actually is

Two techniques share the name, and conflating them causes confusion when you read the literature.

**Classical knowledge distillation** trains the student to match the teacher's full output *distribution*. When the teacher produces a token, it assigns a probability to every possible next token — and that whole distribution carries information the single chosen token does not. "This answer was 70% likely and that one 20%" tells the student that two answers are nearly acceptable, which is a richer signal than "this is the answer."

To use it you need the teacher's **logits**, which means access to its internals.

**Sequence-level distillation**, also called **data distillation**, skips the distribution. You run the teacher, keep the text it generates, and train the student on that text as ordinary supervised data. The signal is sparser — you lose the "70% versus 20%" information — but you need nothing but text.

**Almost all LLM distillation in practice is the second kind**, for a simple reason: if your teacher is behind an API, text is all you will ever have. The distinction is worth knowing so that you recognise which one a paper is describing, and so you do not go looking for logit access you cannot get.

### The workflow

```text
1. REPRESENTATIVE INPUTS
   Real inputs from the workload, as in Phase 3. The student inherits the
   distribution of what it is trained on, including any narrowness.

2. RUN THE TEACHER
   Generate outputs at scale. This is the expensive step -- price it first,
   because it is the one bill distillation definitely incurs.

3. FILTER AND VERIFY          <-- where quality is won or lost
   Remove wrong outputs, degenerate outputs, refusals where an answer was
   expected, formatting failures, and near-duplicates. Verify a sample by hand.

4. TRAIN THE STUDENT
   QLoRA on the filtered set, exactly as in Phase 2.

5. EVALUATE TWICE
   Against the TEACHER (how much did we lose?)
   Against the UNTUNED STUDENT (how much did we gain?)
```

Step 5's two comparisons are not redundant, and doing only one produces a misleading picture. Against the teacher tells you the cost of going cheap. Against the untuned student tells you whether the training did anything at all — and occasionally it did nothing, or made things worse, which you would never discover while looking only at the student's outputs.

### Filtering and verifying — where quality is won

This is the step that decides whether distillation works, and it is the step people compress.

A teacher's raw output is **not** training data. It is a mix that includes the correct, the almost-correct, the confidently wrong, the degenerate (repeated phrases, truncated answers), the unhelpfully verbose, and the systematically duplicated. Training on it unfiltered means training the student to reproduce all of that, and — because the errors are now in the labels — with the authority of ground truth.

**What to filter:**

| Filter | What it removes | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Correctness | Wrong answers | Otherwise you distil the teacher's mistakes deliberately |
| Degenerate output | Repetition, truncation, empty responses | Teaches broken generation patterns |
| Format | Outputs violating your required structure | The student learns inconsistency |
| Duplicates | Near-identical inputs and answers | Overweights whatever repeats |
| Unexplained refusals | Declines where an answer was expected | Teaches refusal as a habit |
| Length outliers | Unusually long or short outputs | Skews the style the student learns |

**Then verify a sample by hand.** Read a random selection of the survivors and confirm the filters did what you think. Filters are code, and code has bugs — a filter that silently removes 40% of correct outputs and keeps the wrong ones will not announce itself. Reading thirty examples takes twenty minutes and is the difference between a dataset and a guess.

⚠️ **The asymmetry to keep in mind:** filtering removes errors that are *present in the data*. It cannot add capability the teacher lacks. If the teacher is wrong about a category of input, and you filter those out rather than correcting them, the student simply never learns that category — which is different from learning it correctly.

### What distillation cannot do

Three limits, each with a practical consequence.

**The student cannot exceed the teacher.** It is learning to imitate, so its ceiling is the teacher's performance on the distilled task. If your teacher scores 82% on your suite, expect the student below that — and treat a student that *appears* to beat the teacher with suspicion, since the usual explanations are a leaked test set or an evaluation that favours the student's narrower style.

**It inherits the teacher's flaws, concentrated.** Errors present in the teacher's outputs on your input distribution become *systematic* in the student, because they are now in every relevant training example rather than being diluted across a general model. A teacher that is 5% wrong on a topic can produce a student that is consistently wrong on it.

**It transfers a task, not general capability.** The student learns the behaviour the data demonstrates. It does not acquire the teacher's breadth, reasoning, or world knowledge — and it will be substantially weaker on anything adjacent to the task. This is fine when the goal is narrow task excellence, which is what Phase 1's winning conditions described all along.

**Diversity collapse** deserves its own mention. Model-generated outputs are more alike than human outputs, so a dataset made entirely of them narrows the range of what the student produces. Aggressive filtering helps, but the effect is real: the student may become accurate on the common case while losing the teacher's ability to handle variation. Watching for it is part of evaluating the student honestly.

### Terms of service — read them, because it is contractual

This is the part of distillation that is not a technical question, and it cannot be resolved by being careful.

**Many commercial providers' terms prohibit using their outputs to train a competing model.** Others permit it with conditions, or permit it entirely. The position varies by provider and changes over time, so there is no answer to memorise — only a step to perform.

**Perform the step:**

1. Find the terms of service for the provider whose outputs you used.
2. Locate the section on using outputs, training, or derivative models.
3. Record the answer, with the **date you checked it**, in your deliverable.
4. If it prohibits what you are doing, stop, and use a provider whose terms permit it, or an open-weight teacher whose licence allows it.

"I did not read it" is not a defence, and the risk is not hypothetical — it is the kind of term that gets enforced against products rather than hobby projects. Open-weight teachers are the safest route, because their licences are explicit and you can read them.

⚠️ **Volatile:** these terms change, sometimes without notice. The date on your record is what makes it useful later.

### The economics, in your own numbers

Now the reason to do any of this. The calculation is about the **easy majority**, not the average.

```text
Your workload has a distribution of difficulty.
The strong model handles it all at cost C_strong per call.
A student handles the EASY fraction f at cost C_student per call, near zero if local.

Cost per call with routing:  f * C_student + (1 - f) * C_strong
Savings:                     C_strong - (f * C_student + (1 - f) * C_strong)
                           = f * (C_strong - C_student)

Break-even volume:           total_savings >= generation_cost + training_cost + maintenance
```

Two things this makes visible.

**Savings scale with the easy fraction, not with the model's price difference.** If only 30% of your traffic is genuinely easy, you save on 30% of calls no matter how cheap the student is. Measuring that fraction is the first thing to do, and it is done with the eval suite from Phase 4 — classify cases by whether the student handles them.

**The upfront costs are real and must be counted.** The teacher generation run is a genuine bill, training the student costs time and GPU hours, and maintenance recurs forever, because — as Phase 1 established — every base-model update re-opens the work. Distillation is not free money; it is trading an ongoing per-call cost for an upfront cost plus a permanent maintenance obligation. That trade is excellent at high volume and poor at low volume, which is exactly what the break-even calculation tells you.

**This pairs with the Cost track.** Distillation produces the cheap model; **routing** decides which calls reach it; **local models** are where the near-zero marginal cost comes from. None of the three is sufficient alone, and together they are how a paid workload becomes affordable.

### When this is the right answer

Distillation is worth doing when all of these hold:

- The workload has **high volume** — enough that per-call savings exceed the upfront cost in a reasonable time.
- A **large easy fraction** — most traffic is routine.
- The task is **narrow and stable** — it is not going to be redefined next month.
- You have an **eval suite** — from Phase 4, so you can measure what you lost.
- Your teacher's **terms permit it**.

That is a genuinely narrower list than "we want to save money", and it is the same shape as Phase 1's conditions. Distillation is not an exception to the earlier advice; it is the identification of the case that advice was pointing at.

## Common Pitfalls

**Training on unfiltered teacher output.** Raw generations include the wrong, the degenerate and the duplicated. Filtering is where the technique is won, and it is the step most often compressed.

**Skipping the hand-verification sample.** Filters are code and code has bugs. Reading thirty survivors takes twenty minutes and catches a filter that is silently keeping the wrong examples.

**Comparing the student only against itself.** Without the teacher comparison you do not know what you lost; without the untuned comparison you do not know whether training did anything.

**Expecting the student to beat the teacher.** It is imitating, so the teacher is the ceiling. A student that appears to exceed it usually means a leaked test set or an evaluation biased toward the student's narrower style.

**Assuming general capability came along.** Distillation transfers a task. The student will be substantially weaker on everything adjacent.

**Ignoring diversity collapse.** Model outputs are more alike than human ones, so the student can narrow. Check for it rather than assuming filtering solved it.

**Ignoring the terms of service.** Many providers restrict training on their outputs, and this is contractual rather than advisory. Record what you checked and when.

**Forgetting maintenance.** Every base-model update re-opens the work. Distillation trades a per-call cost for an upfront cost plus a permanent obligation.

**Distilling at low volume.** If the break-even calculation says the upfront costs never pay back, the answer is no — regardless of how good the student turns out.

## Quiz

### Q1. Which kind of distillation is almost all LLM distillation in practice? <!-- id: ft-05-distillation-q01 energy: normal -->

- [ ] Classical KD, matching the teacher's full output distribution
- [x] Sequence-level or data distillation — training on the teacher's generated text, which needs only text rather than logits
- [ ] Reinforcement learning from human feedback
- [ ] Quantization

**Why:** Classical KD requires the teacher's logits — the probability it assigned to every possible token — which in turn requires access to its internals. If the teacher is behind a commercial API, text is all you will ever receive, so the sequence-level approach is the only one available. It carries a sparser signal, since "this was 70% likely and that 20%" is lost, but it works with any teacher. Knowing the distinction prevents you from looking for logit access you cannot get.

### Q2. Which step decides whether distillation produces a good student? <!-- id: ft-05-distillation-q02 energy: high -->

- [ ] The training hyperparameters
- [ ] The size of the student model
- [x] Filtering and verifying the teacher's outputs — raw generations include wrong, degenerate and duplicated examples that become ground truth if kept
- [ ] The number of teacher outputs generated

**Why:** A teacher's raw output is a mixture, not training data. Because the errors end up in the labels, keeping them means deliberately training the student to reproduce them with the authority of supervised data. Filtering removes the wrong, the degenerate, the duplicated and the malformed, and the hand-verification sample confirms the filters are doing what you assumed — filters are code, and a filter silently keeping the wrong examples will not announce itself.

### Q3. Why must you compare the student against both the teacher and the untuned student? <!-- id: ft-05-distillation-q03 energy: high -->

- [ ] To increase the sample size
- [x] The teacher comparison shows what you lost by going cheap; the untuned comparison shows whether training did anything at all
- [ ] Because the teacher is always better
- [ ] To satisfy licensing requirements

**Why:** The two answer different questions and neither substitutes for the other. Against the teacher you learn the cost of the trade — if the student is twenty points behind, the economics may not justify it. Against the untuned student you learn whether the training had any effect, and occasionally it did nothing or made things worse, which is invisible if you only ever look at the tuned model's outputs. Reporting all three numbers is what turns an experiment into a measurement.

### Q4. What is the ceiling on a distilled student's quality? <!-- id: ft-05-distillation-q04 energy: normal -->

- [ ] The size of its training dataset
- [ ] The number of epochs trained
- [x] The teacher's performance on the same task — it is imitating, so it cannot exceed what it is imitating
- [ ] There is no ceiling

**Why:** Distillation transfers behaviour from teacher to student, so the teacher's performance on the distilled task is the upper bound. That is why a student appearing to beat the teacher deserves scrutiny rather than celebration: the usual explanations are test-set leakage or an evaluation that favours the student's narrower, more consistent style. The ceiling is not a defect — a student two points behind at a fraction of the cost is exactly the intended outcome.

### Q5. Why are teacher errors *more* systematic in a student than in the teacher? <!-- id: ft-05-distillation-q05 energy: high -->

- [ ] Because the student is smaller
- [x] Because the teacher's errors on your input distribution appear in every relevant training example, so the student learns them consistently rather than encountering them occasionally
- [ ] Because filtering removes the correct examples
- [ ] Because the student trains for fewer epochs

**Why:** In a general model, an error on a topic is diluted across a vast range of behaviour — the model is wrong sometimes. In a distilled student trained on inputs drawn from your workload, that same error appears in the relevant examples repeatedly, so it becomes the learned behaviour rather than an occasional lapse. This is why a teacher that is 5% wrong on a category of input can produce a student that is consistently wrong on it, and why filtering correctness rather than only degeneracy matters.

### Q6. What is the most important thing to check about your teacher before generating data? <!-- id: ft-05-distillation-q06 energy: high -->

- [ ] Its benchmark scores
- [ ] Its context window
- [x] Its terms of service on training models with its outputs — many providers prohibit it, and this is contractual rather than advisory
- [ ] Its latency

**Why:** The technical work is reversible; a licensing breach is not. ### Q7. What does distilling save you, and on how much of your traffic? <!-- id: ft-05-distillation-q07 energy: high -->

- [ ] It saves the full cost difference on every call
- [x] It saves on the easy fraction only — savings scale with how much traffic the student can genuinely handle, not with the price difference

If only 30% of traffic is genuinely easy, you save on 30% of calls however cheap the student is. That is why measuring the easy fraction comes first, and why it is done with the eval suite — and why low-volume distillation cannot pay back its upfront generation and training costs regardless of how good the student is.

### Q8. What must distillation trade away for its savings? <!-- id: ft-05-distillation-q08 energy: normal -->

- [ ] Nothing — it is strictly better
- [ ] Only the teacher's latency
- [x] An upfront generation and training cost, plus a permanent maintenance obligation, in exchange for a lower per-call cost

## Free vs Paid

**The technique is free; the teacher may not be.** Unsloth, `peft`, `trl`, `datasets` and `pytest` are open-source and free, and a free GPU session is enough to train a small student. What can cost money is **running the teacher over your inputs**, which is the one unavoidable bill in the workflow — so price it before you start, using task 3.

**The free path, concretely.** Use whatever model access you already have as the teacher, generate a modest dataset on a free tier, filter it carefully, and train a small student on a free GPU. Careful filtering compensates substantially for a weaker teacher, which is a genuine advantage of the free path: the technique rewards the step that costs nothing but attention.

**Where a free teacher is the honest choice, not a compromise.** An **open-weight teacher** avoids the terms-of-service question entirely, since its licence is explicit and you can run it yourself without a per-call bill. You trade some capability for legal clarity and zero marginal cost, and for a narrow task that trade is frequently good. This is the route to prefer when you intend to build something you might ship.

**Honest limits of the free path.** Free-tier teachers have rate limits, so generating a large dataset takes time spread across days rather than one session. Free judges used for filtering are weaker, which is another argument for hand-verifying a sample rather than trusting automated filtering alone. And free GPU sessions disconnect, so the persisted dataset and adapter matter as much here as in Phase 3.

**What paid tiers add, and when they are worth it.** A stronger teacher and faster generation. Spend on a teacher once you have a measured easy fraction and a task that has cleared Phase 1, not before.
