# Phase 3 — Proof of Skill Without a Job

## Goal of this phase

Produce evidence that you can do the work, in the absence of anyone having paid you to do it.

This is the phase that closes the gap most people never close. You can be genuinely capable and completely invisible, because capability that nobody can see does not reduce an employer's risk — and reducing their risk is the entire transaction from Phase 1. A job gives you evidence for free: you did work, someone paid you, someone can vouch for you. **Without a job you have to manufacture that evidence deliberately**, and this phase is the manufacturing process.

The reassuring part is that the substitute is unusually effective in this specific field. Hiring in software has always weighted demonstrated work heavily, and it does so more now, because generation is cheap and the scarce thing is judgement. **Judgement is hard to fake and easy to display** — which means the absence of a job is a real obstacle but not the obstacle people assume.

## Estimated time

**1 week at 1–2 focused hours a day** to learn the procedures and produce the first artefacts. The ongoing work is not a week; it is a habit you run in the background. **The single highest-value change is to start writing in public now rather than after you feel ready**, because feeling ready is not a milestone that arrives.

## Skills you'll gain

- Explain **why evidence of judgement is scarce**, and therefore why a small amount of it is disproportionately valuable.
- **Contribute to an existing open-source project** at an appropriate scale, without wasting maintainers' time.
- **Write a technical explanation** of a mechanism you learned, and use the writing itself to find your own gaps.
- **Build something for one real user**, and understand why that evidence is stronger than another personal project.
- **Reproduce a result** from a paper or a project, which is one of the most legible forms of evidence available and one of the least attempted.
- **Publish consistently enough** to be findable, without turning it into a content-production treadmill.
- Say what each artefact **proves and what it does not**, which is the honesty that makes the rest credible.

## Specific topics to learn

1. **The scarcity argument.** Why everyone can now produce plausible output, and what remains rare.
2. **What counts as evidence.** The difference between an artefact that demonstrates judgement and one that demonstrates activity.
3. **Open-source contribution**, from documentation fixes to small features, and the norms that make maintainers willing to help you.
4. **Writing in public.** Explaining a mechanism, documenting a failure, and why "I built X" is the weakest form of technical writing.
5. **Building for one real user.** Why a single non-you user changes what the work demonstrates.
6. **Reproduction.** Taking someone's claimed result and checking it, and why this is unusually legible.
7. **Consistency over volume.** A publishing cadence you can sustain for a year, chosen against what looks impressive this month.
8. **Making yourself findable.** A profile that points at the work, and the small mechanics that determine whether anyone ever sees it.

## Tools for This Phase

- **A GitHub account** with a real profile README, which you may already have from Phase 2.
- **A place to write** — GitHub Pages, dev.to, Hashnode, a plain markdown file in a repository, or your own site. Any free option is fine and the choice matters less than publishing.
- **An existing open-source project** you actually use. The best candidate is a library you have already hit a rough edge in, because you have a real problem to solve rather than a hypothetical one.
- **`docs/research/vibecoding-tool-landscape.md`** in this repository if you want to see how a researched document marks verified claims against unverified ones — that discipline is what makes written work credible.

Everything here is free. Do not pay for a writing platform, a course on open source, or a portfolio builder.

## Free/cheap resources

- **The contributing guide of any project you use.** Read it before you open anything. Projects that write these are telling you how to succeed, and ignoring them is the most common way a first contribution goes badly.
- **Issues labelled for newcomers.** Many projects tag them explicitly. They are pre-filtered for the maintainer's own view of what is approachable.
- **Your own confusion.** Every time you spend twenty minutes working something out that the documentation did not explain, you have found a contribution and a writing topic at the same time. This is the most reliable source of both, and it is free.
- **Papers and project READMEs with claimed results**, for reproduction. Choose something small and checkable rather than something impressive.
- **`ai-roadmaps/finetuning/00-overview.md`** in this repository, since evaluation and measurement work is the most directly employable evidence and the most accessible entry point from Phase 1's role map.

**Be careful with advice about "building an audience".** A great deal of it optimises for attention rather than for credibility, and they are different things that frequently conflict. **A post that explains one mechanism correctly and admits its limits is worth more to a hiring manager than a post that gets ten times the views and says nothing checkable.**

## Lesson: The Scarcest Thing Is Someone Who Can Tell Good Work From Bad

### Part 1 — Why evidence of judgement is scarce

Start with the arithmetic that makes this phase worth doing.

**Generation became cheap, so the volume of plausible-looking work exploded.** Repositories, posts, projects, portfolios — all of it got easier to produce. Which means the *quantity* of visible work is no longer informative about anything, because everyone has some.

**What is still rare:**

**Someone who can tell whether a result is real.** Most people can produce an output. Far fewer can say whether it is correct, and fewer still can show you how they checked.

**Someone who admits a limitation.** Not performative modesty — a specific statement that a system was tested against these inputs and not those, and here is what that means. The reason this is rare is that it feels like weakness, so almost nobody does it, which is exactly why it works.

**Someone who can write down what they did so a stranger can follow it.** Technical writing is genuinely difficult and most engineers avoid it, which makes competent written work a differentiator rather than a baseline.

**Someone who finished something.** Not started — finished, with the unglamorous parts done.

Notice the pattern: **none of these are about capability in the abstract. All of them are about evidence of judgement.** And judgement is what Phase 1 established employers are actually pricing, because it is the thing that cannot be delegated to a tool.

**So the strategy for this phase is not "produce more work".** It is to produce a small number of artefacts that demonstrate judgement specifically, and to make them findable.

### Part 2 — What counts as evidence, and what only looks like it

The trap is producing activity that reads as effort but carries no information about judgement.

**Things that demonstrate activity:** a new repository per week; a long commit streak; a list of technologies; a certificate; a profile full of "learning in public" without any completed thing.

**Things that demonstrate judgement:**

**A contribution to a project you did not write.** This is strong precisely because the standard was set by someone else, and a maintainer accepted your work. It shows you can operate in an existing codebase under someone else's conventions — which is what a job actually is.

**A written explanation of a mechanism, with its limits stated.** It shows you understood something well enough to explain it, and that you know what you did not verify.

**A measurement.** You defined what correct means and showed a number. This is the single most legible artefact available, because so few people do it.

**A reproduction.** You took someone's claimed result and checked it. See Part 6.

**A thing built for a real user who is not you.** See Part 5.

**The test that separates the two lists:** *does this artefact tell a stranger something about how I make decisions?* If the answer is no, it is activity. A repository with a hundred commits says you were busy. A repository with a README that states the problem, one decision you made, one thing you measured, and one limitation says how you think.

**And the corollary about volume, which is counterintuitive:** three artefacts of the second kind beat thirty of the first. Not because more is worse, but because the first kind dilutes — a reader who finds a wall of tutorial clones has learned nothing about you and has to look harder for the real work. **Curation is part of the evidence.**

### Part 3 — Open-source contribution, at the right scale

This is the most direct substitute for employment, because it is **work in someone else's codebase, reviewed by someone else, and accepted or not** — which is structurally the same as a job, minus the salary.

**Start with a project you actually use.** Not the most popular project, and not one chosen for its résumé value. The reason is practical: you need a real problem, and you have real observations about the software you rely on. This also makes the interaction natural rather than transactional, which maintainers can tell.

**Read the contributing guide before anything else.** It exists, it is usually short, and it tells you the conventions. A first contribution that ignores it reads as someone who did not bother, and that impression is difficult to undo.

**Start smaller than feels satisfying.** Documentation fixes, a clearer error message, a missing test, a typo in a docstring. This is not a lesser contribution — it is a way to learn the project's process, its review culture, and its CI on a change where the stakes are low. **You are buying familiarity cheaply**, and you will make your real contribution better because of it.

**How to open an issue well**, since this is often the first interaction:

**State what you expected and what happened.** Not "it is broken" — the specific difference.

**Give the smallest reproduction.** A few lines of code rather than a description. If you cannot make a small reproduction, say so; that is useful information for the maintainer.

**Say what you already checked.** Version, environment, whether you searched for existing issues. This signals that you are not asking them to do your investigation.

**Separate the bug report from the fix, and ask before doing large work.** A large unrequested pull request on a project you have not contributed to is a common and demoralising mistake — it may not match the direction the maintainer wants, and they now have to reject work someone did for free. **Ask first** with a short proposal.

**The norms that make maintainers willing to help you**, and these matter more than the code:

**Be patient.** Reviews take time, and a polite nudge after a week is fine while a daily ping is not.

**Accept changes to your work without ego.** A maintainer rewriting your contribution is the system working, not a rejection of you. How you respond to review is itself evidence of judgement.

**Do not take silence personally.** Many projects are maintained by one person with a job.

**Say thank you and mean it.** These are volunteers, and the difference in how your next contribution is received is real.

**What this gives you, precisely:** a public, verifiable record that someone with no obligation to you accepted your work. **That is the strongest single artefact in this phase**, and it is free.

### Part 4 — Writing in public, and what to write

Writing is the highest-leverage artefact here for an unglamorous reason: **it scales without a meeting.** A stranger can evaluate your thinking from a document in five minutes, which is exactly the budget a hiring manager has.

**Write explanations, not announcements.** "I built X" tells a reader almost nothing. "How X actually works, and where I got it wrong" tells them how you think. The second is also easier to write well, because you are reporting something you know rather than performing something you made.

**The forms that work, roughly in order of value:**

**A mechanism explained.** Take something you had to work out — how tokenisation splits an unusual string, why a retrieval system returned the wrong document, how a caching scheme actually behaves — and explain it clearly. **The test of understanding is whether you can explain the failure case**, so include one.

**A failure documented.** What you tried, what broke, how you found out, what you changed. This is unusually strong because it demonstrates the debugging process, which is precisely what interviews test and portfolios rarely show.

**A measurement reported.** What you measured, how, what the number was, and what it does not tell you. If you have an evaluation suite from the Finetuning track, this writes itself, and it is the most employable thing many readers will have.

**A reproduction.** See Part 6.

**What to avoid:** listicles, "top ten tools" posts, and anything that could have been written without doing the work. These are not dishonest, but they are indistinguishable from a thousand others and they cost you the credibility that a specific piece earns.

**The underrated technique: write to find your own gaps.** You will discover, midway through explaining a mechanism, that you do not actually know one part of it. **That moment is the value**, not an obstacle. Writing is a test you can apply to yourself before anyone else does, and the gaps it finds are your real curriculum. This connects directly to Safety & Ethics Phase 5's unaided self-assessment.

**On cadence.** Pick something you can sustain for a year rather than something impressive this month. One a month beats four in a fortnight followed by silence, because consistency is itself a signal and because you will produce better work at a pace you can hold. **A blog with four posts and a year of silence reads as an abandoned project**, which is the opposite of the impression you want.

### Part 5 — Building for one real user

The strongest personal-project evidence is work that someone who is not you actually uses. The reason is that a real user introduces constraints you cannot invent: they will use it in ways you did not anticipate, they will hit inputs you did not consider, and they will tell you when it is wrong.

**You do not need a business.** One person is enough, and they can be someone you know.

**Where to find one, at zero cost and zero travel:** a small local business that does something manually; a student who needs a specific tool; a community group; a relative with a repetitive task; an open-source project that needs a small utility. **The best candidate is someone who currently does the thing by hand**, because you can measure the improvement.

**What a real user changes about the work, concretely:**

**It forces you to define correctness.** "It works" becomes "it produces the same result the manual process did, for these twenty cases", which is a measurement.

**It produces requirements you would not have written.** The user asks for something you did not think of, and now you have a real decision to document.

**It creates the failure you have to handle.** They enter something strange, and you find out your validation was missing.

**It gives you a second opinion that is not yours.** You cannot assess your own work's usability, and neither can a model, because both of you know how it is supposed to work.

**And it gives you something to write about.** "I built a tool for a specific person, here is what they asked for that I did not anticipate, and here is what I measured" is a far stronger post than any description of a personal project.

**The honest caveat:** do not take on real responsibility for someone's data or business without understanding what that means. If the tool handles personal information, Safety & Ethics Phase 2 stops being theoretical. A small tool that does not touch personal data is a fine first real-user project.

### Part 6 — Reproduction, the most underrated evidence

Here is an artefact almost nobody produces, and it is unusually legible.

**Take a claimed result — from a paper, a project README, a blog post — and check whether you can reproduce it.** Then write up what you found.

**Why it is so strong:**

**It requires understanding.** You cannot reproduce something you do not understand.

**It produces a number**, which is checkable.

**It demonstrates scepticism**, which is the exact disposition employers are pricing in an era of confident output.

**The finding is interesting either way.** If you reproduce it, you have confirmed something and can say under what conditions. If you cannot, you have found either a limitation of your setup or a problem with the claim, and **saying which — and how you would tell the difference — is itself the demonstration.**

**Practical guidance:** choose something small and checkable rather than something impressive. A single benchmark number on a small model, one claimed improvement from a modest paper, or a library's stated performance characteristic. **Document your environment and versions**, because a failed reproduction that turns out to be a version difference is still a useful contribution when you say so.

**The honest framing, which is what makes it credible:** a failed reproduction is not a refutation. Say what you ran, what you got, and what you would need to distinguish "the claim is wrong" from "my setup differs". **That sentence is the whole artefact**, and it is the kind of thing that makes a reader trust everything else you write.

### Part 7 — Being findable, and the small mechanics

Evidence nobody finds is not evidence. This part is short and unglamorous and most people skip it.

**A profile README on your main GitHub profile** that says what you work on, what you are looking for, and links to your three best artefacts. This is the page a stranger lands on.

**Pin your best repositories.** Most profiles show whatever you pushed last, which is usually the least representative work. Pinning is a two-minute fix for a real problem.

**Put the artefact in the README, not behind a link.** The problem statement, one decision, one measurement, one limitation — in the repository's own README. A reviewer should not have to click twice.

**Make the writing reachable from the profile.** If your posts live somewhere else, link them where a visitor will see them.

**State what you are looking for.** "Looking for junior or evaluation-focused work, remote, UTC+8" is a sentence that turns a passive profile into an approachable one. Visitors do not guess.

**Do not optimise beyond this.** Keyword-stuffing, follower counts and engagement tactics are aimed at a different problem. **The goal is that a specific person, arriving from a job application or a search, finds your best work in under two minutes.** That is achievable in an afternoon and it is where the return is.

## Common Pitfalls

**Producing volume instead of evidence.** Thirty tutorial-shaped repositories tell a reviewer less than three pieces of real work, and they make the real work harder to find. Curation is part of the artefact.

**Waiting until you feel ready to write.** Readiness does not arrive. The first post will be the worst one and it is still worth publishing, because the alternative is a blank profile.

**Contributing to impress rather than because you use the project.** Maintainers can tell, and the work is worse because you do not have a real problem to solve.

**Opening a large pull request on a project you have never contributed to.** It is the most common way a first contribution goes badly. Ask first, with a short proposal.

**Taking review personally.** A maintainer rewriting your change is the system working. How you respond to it is itself evidence, and it is being observed.

**Writing announcements instead of explanations.** "I built X" is indistinguishable from ten thousand others. "How X works and where I got it wrong" is not.

**Choosing a reproduction that is impressive rather than checkable.** The value is in careful reporting, not in the size of the claim, and an uncheckable one produces nothing you can write about honestly.

**Building for a hypothetical user.** "People would want this" is not a user. One real person who actually uses it changes what the work demonstrates, even if that person is your neighbour.

**Letting the writing become content production.** Optimising for views pulls you toward listicles and away from the specificity that makes the work credible. Those are different goals and they conflict.

**Leaving the evidence unfindable.** Excellent work behind three clicks on an unpinned repository is work a reviewer will not see. The mechanics take an afternoon.

## Quiz

### Q1. Why does a contribution to a project you did not write carry more weight than another personal project? <!-- id: cr-03-proof-of-skill-without-a-job-q01 energy: normal -->

- [ ] Because open-source projects are more prestigious
- [x] Because the standard was set by someone else and a maintainer accepted your work
- [ ] Because personal projects are usually unfinished
- [ ] Because maintainers write recommendations for contributors

**Why:** The value is that the judgement about whether the work was good enough came from outside you. A personal project is assessed entirely on your own terms; a merged contribution means someone with no obligation to you looked at your work in their codebase and took it. That is structurally the same evidence a job would provide.

### Q2. You spend twenty minutes working something out that the documentation did not explain. What have you found? <!-- id: cr-03-proof-of-skill-without-a-job-q02 energy: normal -->

- [ ] A reason to stop using the library
- [ ] A bug report to file immediately
- [x] Both a potential contribution and a writing topic
- [ ] Evidence that the project is poorly maintained

**Why:** Confusion that no existing document resolves is the reliable source of both artefact types this phase asks for: a documentation contribution the maintainer will likely welcome, and an explanation you are unusually qualified to write because you just did the work of understanding it. It is also the cheapest source, because you were going to spend the twenty minutes anyway.

### Q3. What is the strongest form of technical writing for a portfolio, according to this phase? <!-- id: cr-03-proof-of-skill-without-a-job-q03 energy: normal -->

- [ ] A listicle of tools you have used
- [ ] An announcement that you built something
- [x] An explanation of a mechanism including where you got it wrong
- [ ] A tutorial following an official guide

**Why:** An explanation with a failure case demonstrates that you understood the mechanism well enough to know its limits, which is the judgement employers are pricing. Announcements and listicles could have been written without doing the work, so they carry almost no information about how you think, and they are indistinguishable from a great many others.

### Q4. Why is a failed reproduction still a valuable artefact? <!-- id: cr-03-proof-of-skill-without-a-job-q04 energy: high -->

- [ ] Because it proves the original claim was false
- [ ] Because it shows persistence
- [ ] Because it is easier than a successful one
- [x] Because reporting what you ran and how you would distinguish a wrong claim from a differing setup is itself the demonstration

**Why:** A failed reproduction is not a refutation, and saying so is what makes the writeup credible. The artefact is the careful reporting: what you ran, what you got, and what you would need in order to tell "the claim is wrong" from "my environment differs". That reasoning demonstrates scepticism and precision, which is exactly the disposition this field now selects for.

### Q5. You want to contribute a substantial feature to a project you have never contributed to. What should you do first? <!-- id: cr-03-proof-of-skill-without-a-job-q05 energy: normal -->

- [ ] Open the pull request and let the maintainer decide
- [x] Ask first with a short proposal
- [ ] Fork the project and build it in your own copy
- [ ] Find a different project with fewer contributors

**Why:** A large unrequested pull request may not match the direction the maintainer wants, which forces them to decline work someone did for free — bad for you and worse for them. A short proposal costs them a minute and tells you whether the work is wanted. This is also the norm the contributing guide of most projects states explicitly.

### Q6. Which of these demonstrates judgement rather than activity? <!-- id: cr-03-proof-of-skill-without-a-job-q06 energy: normal -->

- [ ] A repository with a long commit streak
- [ ] A list of technologies you have touched
- [ ] Ten tutorial projects completed
- [x] A writeup stating the problem, one decision, one measurement and one limitation

**Why:** The other three show that you were busy, which is no longer informative now that production is cheap. The writeup shows how you make decisions: what you chose, what you checked, and what you know is missing. That is the information a reader needs in order to judge whether you can be trusted with a system.

## You're ready to move on when...

- You can explain **why evidence of judgement is scarce**, in terms of what became cheap rather than in terms of effort.
- You can classify your own artefacts into **judgement and activity**, and you have done it strictly.
- You have **submitted something to a project you did not write**, and you can describe what the review taught you.
- You have **published an explanation of a mechanism**, including its failure case.
- You have used writing to find **a gap in your own understanding**, and you kept the list.
- You have **built something for one real user** who is not you, or completed a reproduction.
- You can state **what a failed reproduction does and does not prove**.
- A stranger arriving at your profile can find your **three best artefacts in under two minutes**, and knows what you are looking for.

If every artefact you have is a personal project, this phase has not finished. **The distinguishing feature of the evidence here is that someone other than you set or checked the standard**, and nothing on that list substitutes for it.

## Free vs Paid

**This phase costs nothing at all, and it is the phase where that is least limiting.** Every artefact it asks for is free to produce and free to publish.

**What free gives you, and it is the whole thing:** GitHub, a static site or any writing platform, an existing open-source project to contribute to, one real user, and a claim you can attempt to reproduce. There is no version of this phase that requires money.

**What you genuinely give up:** conference talks and in-person meetups, which are a real path to visibility that costs travel. **The substitute is the written trail** — posts, contributions, and a profile a stranger can evaluate — and for the roles in Phase 1's accessible tier, that substitute is adequate.

**Where paid would change almost nothing:** paid writing platforms, portfolio builders, courses on contributing to open source. All of them package something available free, and none of them produces the artefacts — only you do.

**The one place a small spend could matter later**, and it belongs in a later phase rather than this one: if a project genuinely needs an API key or a domain to be presented properly, that is a specific problem with a small specific cost. **Buy it when it solves that problem, not in advance.** A paid course promising employability is the opposite: it solves a problem you do not have, because the evidence that actually works is free and you have to make it yourself.
